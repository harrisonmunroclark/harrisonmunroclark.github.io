Does the Corporate Parent Matter, Really? Evidence from U.S. Banking

Job Market Paper · Dissertation Chapter 3

Abstract
Does a small corporate effect indicate that parents differ little in the value they add to their subsidiaries? The corporate effect captures differences in subsidiary performance across parents, but those differences have two possible sources: variation in the value parents add and variation in the persistent performance of the subsidiaries they own. I distinguish these two sources by estimating a movers model on U.S. banking data from 1971 to 1998. Because state and federal laws often barred a parent from merging an acquired bank into its other banks, the same bank can be observed under different parents during this period.

I find that parents differ substantially in the value they add and in the persistent performance of the banks they own. Parents that add more value systematically acquire banks with lower persistent performance. The two sources therefore offset one another, reducing observed performance differences across parents. In changes of parent that were planned while still legally blocked, bank profitability begins moving toward that of the incoming parent's other banks only after the parent gains control.

I theorize that parents add value by absorbing subsidiaries' recurring administrative work, freeing their scarce managerial services for expansion. To the extent that parents that add more value take on subsidiaries with lower persistent performance, the corporate effect understates how much parents differ in the value they add. This paper recasts the long-running debate over the magnitude of the corporate effect by shifting attention to the processes that generate it, and contributes to renewed interest in the corporate parent as a central actor in corporate strategy.
