I am a PhD candidate in Strategic Management at HEC Paris and am on the 2026–27 academic job market.

My research focuses on core questions in corporate strategy, particularly how and under what conditions corporate parents add value to the subsidiaries they own. Methodologically, I use both computational models and large-scale empirical datasets. Theoretically, I am interested in conceptualizing organizations in terms of the functions managers perform.

Published Papers
Common Purpose Advantage: Reviving a Managerial Theory of the Firm?

with Rodolphe Durand

Strategic Management Journal, 47(2): 583–615, 2026

| Paper | Media Coverage
What is the most effective way to distribute organizational objectives across managers? While prior work suggests managers should each focus on a single objective, we draw on Barnard's original insights on corporate purpose to identify conditions when managers pursuing the full set of objectives is advantageous. Using a computational model, we find that moderate strategic diversity amongst managers enables practice sharing to generate sufficiently valuable distant search to offset the additional complexity incurred during local search, creating a “common purpose advantage.” The advantage is stronger with fewer objectives, moderate objective correlation, less diversification, and moderate turbulence. Under other conditions, it dissipates or reverses. This work unifies scattered findings on multi-objective firms, contributes to diversification research, and revives interest in the Managerial Theory of the Firm.
Working Papers
Does the Corporate Parent Matter, Really? Evidence from U.S. Banking

Job Market Paper

Does a small corporate effect indicate that parents differ little in the value they add to their subsidiaries? The corporate effect captures differences in subsidiary performance across parents, but those differences have two possible sources: variation in the value parents add and variation in the persistent performance of the subsidiaries they own. I distinguish these two sources by estimating a movers model on U.S. banking data from 1971 to 1998. Because state and federal laws often barred a parent from merging an acquired bank into its other banks, the same bank can be observed under different parents during this period. I find that parents differ substantially in the value they add and in the persistent performance of the banks they own. Parents that add more value systematically acquire banks with lower persistent performance. The two sources therefore offset one another, reducing observed performance differences across parents. In changes of parent that were planned while still legally blocked, bank profitability begins moving toward that of the incoming parent's other banks only after the parent gains control. I theorize that parents add value by absorbing subsidiaries' recurring administrative work, freeing their scarce managerial services for expansion. To the extent that parents that add more value take on subsidiaries with lower persistent performance, the corporate effect understates how much parents differ in the value they add. This paper recasts the long-running debate over the magnitude of the corporate effect by shifting attention to the processes that generate it, and contributes to renewed interest in the corporate parent as a central actor in corporate strategy.
